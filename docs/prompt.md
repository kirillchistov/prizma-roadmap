## TASK
Design an AI-native, data-driven, full-cycle marketing agency and the martech platform that powers it.

## ROLE
Act as an integrated founding team:
- CEO and venture strategist;
- CMO and full-funnel agency operator;
- enterprise CPG marketing and retail-media specialist;
- growth experimentation and lifecycle marketing lead;
- marketing scientist specialising in causal inference and econometrics;
- CTO, AI systems architect, and data-platform architect;
- product and UX lead;
- CFO and commercial operations lead;
- privacy, security, and AI-governance lead.

Your task is to produce decision-grade grounding material for creating a martech startup: a coherent business thesis, customer strategy, operating model, product requirements, architecture, economic model, validation plan, and staged implementation roadmap.

Do not merely list tools or describe an agency with AI features. Explain how the business creates, delivers, measures, and captures value.

## FOUNDING AMBITION
Build a highly automated, AI-powered, data-driven, full-cycle marketing agency, supported by a reusable software platform.

Its operating loop should connect:
Business objectives → customer and market research → diagnosis → strategy →
financial and marketing planning → creative and content → activation →
sales and customer outcomes → causal measurement → learning →
the next strategy, creative brief, experiment, and budget decision.

“Full-cycle” means ownership of the agreed marketing workflow and its interfaces with sales, commerce, product, and customer success. It does not mean assuming control of functions the client or its partners retain.

“Fully automated” is a long-term design ambition, not an untested claim.
Distinguish:
1. autonomous execution within pre-approved policies;
2. execution requiring explicit approval;
3. human-led decisions supported by AI;
4. tasks that cannot responsibly or economically be automated yet.

Do not assume all clients need the same service model or software interface.

## TARGET SEGMENTS
Evaluate three candidate segments:

1. Enterprise CPG brands:
   - global brands;
   - top-five local brands in the ten largest CPG markets.
   Define “largest CPG market” and “top-five local brand” before producing rankings.
   Clarify whether leadership means category sales, overall CPG sales, market share,
   brand value, or another measure.
   Distinguish brands, brand-owning companies, categories, and countries.
   Do not silently substitute advertising-spend rankings for CPG-market size.
   If comparable evidence is unavailable, state the limitation and propose a
   defensible proxy.

2. Growing medium-sized companies:
   - an established, commercially viable product offering;
   - an existing customer base;
   - a need for more predictable and profitable growth.
   Treat “sustainable product offering” provisionally as commercial durability,
   not environmental sustainability, unless clarified.
   Propose measurable eligibility criteria rather than inventing fixed thresholds.

3. High-growth opportunity segments:
   - companies in actively expanding categories;
   - a strong product or credible evidence of product–market fit;
   - a clear need for growth experimentation and execution.
   This is a discovery category, not a finished ICP.
   Propose and compare concrete verticals and business models.
   Separate growth-hacking support from short-lived tactics, manipulation,
   platform-policy violations, or growth that destroys unit economics.

These segments may overlap. Establish a classification rule to prevent double
counting and distinguish company size, category growth, product maturity, and
marketing need.

## PRIOR DISCUSSION CONTEXT
Use the following as working context from earlier founder discussions, not as
validated evidence for every segment:

- Clients want business outcomes and clarity, not another collection of tools.
- Fragmented research, strategy, creative, media, analytics, and CRM workflows
  prevent learning from changing execution.
- Some owners and managers lack time, specialist capacity, and confidence in
  marketing decisions.
- Complex attribution and channel reporting can obscure whether marketing
  generates incremental business value.
- Technical sophistication should sit behind a simple experience that explains:
  what happened, why it matters, what happens next, and what requires approval.
- A useful dashboard should connect actions to money and business outcomes,
  rather than merely display activity metrics.
- A defensible opportunity may lie in cross-platform orchestration, data quality,
  independent measurement, and converting evidence into accountable action,
  rather than duplicating ad-platform interfaces.
- Enterprise buyers may have materially different needs from SMB owners.
  Do not transfer SMB interview findings to CPG enterprises without validation.

## RESEARCH AND REASONING RULES
1. Separate:
   - founder-provided context;
   - independently sourced facts;
   - hypotheses;
   - assumptions;
   - recommendations;
   - unresolved questions.
2. Use current, dated, attributable sources for external factual claims.
3. Distinguish vendor claims from independently demonstrated capabilities.
4. Never invent interviews, market sizes, customer budgets, conversion rates,
   integration capabilities, commercial terms, or performance improvements.
5. When evidence is unavailable, propose how to obtain it.
6. Give calculations, formulas, assumptions, and sensitivity ranges.
7. Do not confuse correlation, attributed conversions, modelled contribution,
   experimental lift, and incremental profit.
8. Prefer an explicit trade-off and a testable recommendation to generic advice.
9. Challenge the premise if an agency, SaaS, or hybrid model is unsuitable.
10. Treat all suggested targets as hypotheses unless validated.
11. Never imply access to private conversations, customer datasets, or documents
    that have not actually been supplied or retrieved.

## REQUIRED WORK

### A. CLARIFY THE FOUNDING DECISIONS
Begin with no more than eight high-impact questions that could materially change
the strategy. Cover launch geography, founder assets, budget, team, customer
access, commercial model, autonomy limits, and initial scope.

If answers are unavailable, continue using a clearly labelled assumptions table.
Do not stop with questions alone.

### B. CUSTOMER NEEDS AND SEGMENT SELECTION
For each segment:
- identify economic buyer, champion, users, procurement, and blockers;
- describe jobs to be done and current alternatives;
- distinguish expressed requests from underlying needs;
- map pains to financial consequences and buying triggers;
- assess data availability, implementation burden, willingness to pay,
  sales-cycle complexity, and required trust;
- identify disqualifying conditions;
- propose problem-interview questions and observable validation evidence.

Create a pain-to-value matrix:
Segment | buyer | job | pain | current workaround | business impact |
proposed capability | required data | proof of value | confidence.

Use explicit weighted criteria to compare attractiveness.
Show sensitivity to different founder resources and priorities.
Recommend one beachhead ICP, one initial use case, and the segments to defer.

### C. POSITIONING AND BUSINESS MODEL
Compare:
- AI-native managed agency;
- managed-service-plus-platform hybrid;
- software-first platform;
- relevant partnership or white-label models.

Recommend the initial model and its likely evolution.
Define the customer promise, boundaries of responsibility, differentiation,
substitution risks, and reasons to buy rather than build.

Evaluate competition from:
- agency holding groups and their platforms;
- independent and specialist agencies;
- ad-platform automation;
- martech point solutions;
- internal client teams;
- emerging AI-native operators.

Define a plausible moat, its formation mechanism, time to develop, and ways it
could fail. Do not call generic model access or a collection of integrations a moat.

### D. FULL-CYCLE SERVICE AND OPERATING MODEL
Map the workflow from onboarding to renewal:
- commercial and data-readiness diagnosis;
- research, customer insight, segmentation, and positioning;
- strategy, forecasting, and budget allocation;
- creative briefing, production, localisation, and quality assurance;
- paid, owned, earned, commerce, and lifecycle activation where relevant;
- landing pages and conversion optimisation;
- CRM and sales handoff where applicable;
- measurement, experiments, optimisation, and reporting.

For CPG, explicitly consider retailer and distributor dependencies, sell-in versus
sell-out, retail media, offline sales, promotion, availability, pricing, brand
building, and incomplete access to consumer-level data.

For every workflow specify:
Trigger → inputs → decision logic → execution → output → owner →
approval rule → success metric → failure handling → learning feedback.

Separate the repeatable platform core from segment-specific workflows and
custom-service work. Explain how to avoid becoming an unscalable consultancy.

### E. MEASUREMENT AND DECISION SCIENCE
Design a measurement system appropriate to each segment’s data maturity.

Specify when to use:
- business and financial reporting;
- attribution for operational diagnostics;
- controlled experiments and incrementality testing;
- marketing mix modelling;
- forecasting and scenario planning;
- qualitative evidence.

Define data sufficiency, assumptions, uncertainty, confounders, and failure modes.
Do not prescribe MMM or rapid experimentation when data volume, history, or
statistical power is inadequate.

Connect measurement to actual decisions:
What evidence changes which budget, audience, creative, offer, or channel choice?
Who approves it? How quickly? How will the change be evaluated?

Address brand and performance time horizons, contribution margin, returns,
discounts, stockouts, sales capacity, and customer quality.
Distinguish causal marketing impact from responsibility for total company growth.

### F. PRODUCT AND UX
Define:
- product vision and primary user roles;
- onboarding and minimum data-readiness assessment;
- shared business goals and constraints;
- planning and scenario workspace;
- campaign and experiment workflows;
- approval inbox and exception handling;
- executive dashboard and decision history;
- agency operations console.

Design around progressive disclosure: simple decisions first, inspectable depth
underneath.

Show three concrete user journeys:
1. enterprise brand or regional marketing lead;
2. medium-sized company owner or marketing director;
3. growth lead at a high-growth company.

Describe their differences rather than forcing one interface on everyone.

### G. TECHNICAL AND AI ARCHITECTURE
Provide a text architecture diagram covering:
- connectors and ingestion;
- identity, permissions, consent, and tenant isolation;
- canonical data model and metric definitions;
- data quality, lineage, and freshness;
- analytical and causal models;
- client-specific knowledge and brand assets;
- workflow orchestration and durable execution;
- AI reasoning, retrieval, and bounded tool use;
- deterministic policy and budget controls;
- execution adapters;
- evaluations, observability, audit trails, and incident response.

Distinguish tasks suited to rules, optimisation algorithms, statistical models,
LLMs, and humans. Do not assume every workflow needs a multi-agent system.

For each proposed AI role specify:
objective, inputs, tools, permissions, outputs, evaluation, escalation, and owner.

Include protection against prompt injection, cross-client data leakage,
hallucinated claims, unsafe content, duplicate execution, budget overruns,
platform API changes, and failed integrations.

Use simulation, shadow mode, approval gates, idempotency, rollback where possible,
and compensating actions where rollback is impossible.

Compare build, buy, partner, and open-source options.
Verify critical API availability and commercial constraints before relying on them.
Do not select a stack solely because the founders already know it.

### H. AUTONOMY AND GOVERNANCE
Create an autonomy matrix by action and client type.

Examples:
- generating a draft;
- publishing content;
- launching a campaign;
- changing bids or budgets;
- changing claims, offers, or prices;
- exporting or deleting customer data.

For each specify allowed scope, budget or risk limits, approval requirements,
monitoring, reversibility, and escalation.

Explain how permission is granted, reviewed, revoked, and audited.
Differentiate recommendations from real external execution.

Identify launch-market legal and contractual questions requiring qualified review.
Do not present generic compliance checklists as legal clearance.

### I. COMMERCIAL MODEL AND UNIT ECONOMICS
Compare retainers, subscriptions, usage pricing, implementation fees, hybrid fees,
and carefully bounded performance incentives.

Model:
- agency fees separately from media spend;
- revenue separately from pass-through billings;
- onboarding and integration costs;
- human delivery and support;
- software, data, inference, and creative-production costs;
- gross and contribution margins;
- acquisition costs, sales cycle, retention, and payback;
- working capital and liability exposure.

Provide downside, base, and upside scenarios with transparent assumptions.
Explain conflicts of interest in media-linked and performance-based pricing.
Do not promise outcome guarantees beyond the startup’s control.

### J. MVP, VALIDATION, AND ROADMAP
Define:
- the smallest paid pilot that tests the core value proposition;
- manual or concierge tasks allowed before automation;
- P0, P1, and deferred capabilities;
- essential integrations and data dependencies;
- acceptance criteria and success measures;
- stop, revise, and scale gates.

Produce:
- a first-30-days discovery and validation plan;
- a 90-day MVP and pilot plan;
- a 12-month staged roadmap;
- a feedback / lead capture form for the reader

Separate:
1. evidence needed to justify building;
2. product delivery;
3. customer onboarding and commercial validation.

Include team roles, effort ranges, dependency sequencing, cost assumptions,
and the operational path from one client to ten and then one hundred.

### K. GROUNDING MATERIAL AND NEXT ACTIONS
Finish with:
- a one-page founder thesis;
- a decision log with rationale;
- an assumptions register ranked by risk;
- a customer-research plan;
- a prioritised product and technical backlog;
- a pilot offer and draft statement of work;
- a reusable client-intake questionnaire;
- an evidence register;
- the five most important next decisions.
